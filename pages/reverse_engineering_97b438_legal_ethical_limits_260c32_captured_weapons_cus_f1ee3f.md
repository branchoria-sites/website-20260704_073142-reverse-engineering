## Introduction

A captured missile fragment, drone wreck, artillery round, or guidance module is not merely a source of technical intelligence. It can also be legal evidence. If preserved correctly, battlefield debris may help establish who manufactured a weapon, how it was supplied, whether [sanctions]({{ 'sanctions/' | relative_url }}) were evaded, which military unit used it, and whether it was involved in a potential war crime. If handled carelessly, that evidential value can be lost permanently. The chain of custody—the documented record of who collected, handled, transferred, examined, and stored an item—is therefore a critical safeguard whenever captured military technology is subjected to [reverse engineering]({{ 'reverse-engineering-foreign-military/' | relative_url }}). A robust custody trail allows technical exploitation and legal accountability to proceed together rather than undermining each other.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIChain of Custody</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It is necessary to assure the court of law that the evidence is authentic, ie ...Read more</span></span></span>

## What Battlefield Debris Can Prove

Modern weapons often contain far more information than their outward appearance suggests. [Fragments]({{ 'fragments/' | relative_url }}) may preserve serial numbers, production marks, electronic components, manufacturing dates, software storage media, or unique engineering characteristics. Investigators can use these details to identify a weapon's origin, trace procurement pathways, reconstruct attack events, or connect matériel to specific supply networks. Organisations specialising in arms tracing routinely use markings, packaging details, and component analysis to identify weapons and ammunition flows across conflict zones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.conflictarm.com/publications/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: conflictarm.com">[conflictarm.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">conflictarm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Conflict Armament ResearchPUBLICATIONSA guide to identifying weapons and ammunition produced by the Democratic People&#x27;s Republic of Korea...</span></span></span>

The evidential value of captured technology extends beyond attribution of manufacture. A missile fragment recovered from a strike site may help answer several legally significant questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the weapon consistent with reports from witnesses?
* Did components originate from sanctioned suppliers or restricted export channels?
* Was the munition modified after manufacture?
* Does damage analysis support or contradict claims about targeting?
* Can recovered parts be linked to other attacks or weapons caches?

</div>

These questions often become important in criminal investigations, sanctions enforcement efforts, and international humanitarian law inquiries. Once evidential integrity is compromised, later technical findings may be challenged as unreliable regardless of their scientific merit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unodc.org/cld/zh/education/tertiary/firearms/module-8/key-issues/firearms-as-evidence.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unodc.org">[unodc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unodc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Firearms Module 8 Key Issues: Firearms as EvidenceA successful firearms trace will identify the manufacturer and the first retail pu...</span></span></span>

## Why Informal Recovery Creates Legal and Safety Risks

The most common threat to evidential value is not sophisticated tampering but informal handling. Soldiers may remove components as souvenirs. Civilians may collect fragments from impact sites. Contractors may disassemble systems before documentation is complete. Each undocumented intervention introduces uncertainty about the item's condition and provenance.

Chain-of-custody doctrine exists precisely to prevent these uncertainties. The central requirement is continuity: investigators must be able to show where an item came from, who possessed it, when transfers occurred, and what actions were performed on it. Courts and investigative bodies rely on this documented continuity to determine whether evidence remained authentic and untampered with after recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIChain of Custody</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It is necessary to assure the court of law that the evidence is authentic, ie ...Read more</span></span></span>

For captured weapons, the risks are amplified because debris often contains hazardous [materials]({{ 'materials/' | relative_url }}):


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Unexploded explosive elements.
* Toxic propellants or residues.
* Sensitive electronics.
* Classified military technologies.
* Fragile forensic traces such as fingerprints, residue patterns, or impact marks.

</div>

A technician eager to inspect electronics may inadvertently destroy residues needed for a later forensic examination. Likewise, an intelligence unit that strips a guidance package before documentation may eliminate evidence needed to reconstruct an attack sequence. The resulting dispute may not concern whether the analysis was technically correct, but whether the evidence can still be trusted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.college.police.uk/docs/appref/Firearm-related-crime-scenes.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.college.police.uk">[police.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.college.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College Police LibraryFirearm related crime scenesIn addition to making any firearm safe, recovering trace evidence, and weapon recovery...</span></span></span>

## How the Custody Trail Works in Practice

The chain of custody begins at the moment of recovery, not when an item reaches a laboratory. Investigators typically document the recovery location, date, time, condition of the object, identifying marks, and the identity of the collector. Photographs, field notes, maps, and evidence labels become part of the record. The item is then packaged, sealed, and assigned a unique identifier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irmct.org/specials/prosecution-evidence-collection/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irmct.org">[irmct.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irmct.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THE PROSECUTION&#x27;S EVIDENCE COLLECTIONThe term &#x27;chain of custody&#x27; refers to the procedures established to record the handling of a pi...</span></span></span>

A complete custody trail generally records:

1. **Recovery** – where and by whom the item was found.
2. **Initial documentation** – photographs, coordinates, condition reports, and markings.
3. **Packaging and sealing** – measures preventing contamination or substitution.
4. **Transfers** – every movement between units, laboratories, warehouses, or investigators.
5. **Analysis activities** – disassembly, testing, imaging, sampling, or reverse engineering.
6. **Storage conditions** – secure facilities and access controls.

7. **Final disposition** – retention, court presentation, archival storage, or destruction. [NCBI+2Komprise](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/)

Each transfer creates a new record. The goal is not merely administrative completeness; it is the ability to reconstruct the item's entire history and demonstrate that no unauthorised person could have altered it without detection. [NCBI+2National Institute of Justice](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/)

## The Tension Between Intelligence Exploitation and Evidence Preservation

Reverse engineering programmes often seek rapid access to captured technology. Engineers may want immediate disassembly, software extraction, materials testing, or component harvesting. Evidence preservation, however, favours controlled handling and detailed documentation before intrusive examination begins.

This creates a recurring tension. The same missile guidance computer may simultaneously be:

* An intelligence source.
* A potential exhibit in a criminal case. [* Evidence for sanctions investigations.](#endnote-5 "
<details class="endnote-snippet"><summary>Source snippet</summary><p>cts collected by the military as evidence in civilian investigations and prosecutions.Read more&quot;)...</p></details>
* A subject for technical exploitation.

International discussions on battlefield evidence increasingly recognise this dual-use problem. Guidance developed for battlefield evidence collection emphasises that materials gathered by military forces may later become evidence in civilian or international proceedings. As a result, collection procedures must preserve both intelligence value and evidential integrity. [IIJ](https://theiij.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/USG-Non-Binding-Guiding-Principles-on-Use-of-Battlefield-Evidence-EN-1.pdf)

The challenge is not to prevent technical examination but to ensure that examination itself becomes part of the documented custody record. When analysts remove components, conduct tests, or extract data, those actions must be logged so later investigators can understand precisely how the item changed during analysis. [NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/)

## Preserving Evidence Without Exposing Sensitive Technology

A common misconception is that evidence preservation requires public disclosure of sensitive military information. In practice, investigators can protect both evidential integrity and security interests.

Several measures help achieve this balance:

* **Controlled access:** only authorised personnel handle the item.
* **Tamper-evident packaging:** seals reveal unauthorised access attempts.
* **Layered documentation:** public case records can be separated from classified technical findings.
* **Digital inventories:** photographs, scans, and metadata preserve information before intrusive testing.
* **Secure storage:** access logs record who entered storage facilities and when. [NCBI+2National Institute of Justice](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/)

The principle is straightforward: secrecy should not replace documentation. Even when technical details remain classified, investigators still need a verifiable record showing that evidence remained under accountable control throughout its lifecycle. [International Criminal Court](https://www.icc-cpi.int/sites/default/files/Publications/Rules-of-Procedure-and-Evidence.pdf)

## Why Custody Matters for Accountability

In disputes over military technology, the strongest technical finding can be weakened by a weak custody trail. A recovered component may appear to prove foreign manufacture, illicit supply, or weapon use in a particular incident. Yet if investigators cannot establish where the item came from or who handled it, opponents can challenge its authenticity.

That is why chain of custody occupies a unique position in the legal and ethical limits surrounding captured technology. It is the mechanism that allows a piece of battlefield debris to move from wreckage to evidence without losing credibility. Reverse engineering can reveal how a weapon works, but only a documented custody trail can reliably show that the object being analysed is the same object recovered from the battlefield. In conflicts where accountability, sanctions enforcement, and attribution are contested, that distinction often matters as much as the technical analysis itself. [Eurojust+3NCBI+3IRMCT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/)
