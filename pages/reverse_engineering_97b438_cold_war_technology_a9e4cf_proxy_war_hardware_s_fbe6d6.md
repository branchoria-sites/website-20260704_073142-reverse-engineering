## Introduction

The Cold War’s most valuable hardware discoveries often did not come from direct confrontations between the United States and the Soviet Union. Instead, they emerged from wars fought by allies, clients and proxies. Korea, Vietnam and the Arab–Israeli conflicts became unintended supply lines for foreign military technology, exposing aircraft, missiles, radars and armoured vehicles that would otherwise have remained inaccessible behind the Iron Curtain or within Western arsenals.

For intelligence agencies and military engineers, proxy wars solved a practical problem. Obtaining an adversary’s equipment through espionage or battlefield seizure inside enemy territory was difficult and politically risky. Client-state wars, by contrast, regularly left damaged, abandoned or captured systems available for inspection. These conflicts transformed regional battlefields into laboratories for [reverse engineering]({{ 'reverse-engineering-foreign-military/' | relative_url }}), technical intelligence and countermeasure development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence/2018-01-31/scavenging-intelligence-us-governments-secret-search-foreign-objects-during-cold-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive The U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government&#x27;s Secret Search for Foreign Objects...31 Jan 2018 — This CIA Directorate of Intelligence report set forth the results of the...</span></span></span>

## Why Client-State Wars Exposed Equipment

Proxy wars created conditions that direct superpower confrontation rarely did. Weapons supplied to allies were deployed in large numbers, operated under combat conditions and sometimes captured intact. When one side overran an opponent’s positions, it often inherited stockpiles of foreign-made equipment together with manuals, [spare parts]({{ 'spare-parts/' | relative_url }}) and support systems.

This mattered because military technology cannot be fully understood from photographs or intercepted communications alone. Engineers wanted access to guidance electronics, radar components, manufacturing techniques and [maintenance]({{ 'maintenance/' | relative_url }}) practices. Captured hardware revealed not only what a weapon could do, but how reliably it could do it and what [weaknesses]({{ 'weaknesses/' | relative_url }}) might be exploited. Declassified American foreign materiel exploitation programmes show a systematic effort to acquire and analyse foreign systems recovered through third-party conflicts rather than direct US–Soviet combat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence/2018-01-31/scavenging-intelligence-us-governments-secret-search-foreign-objects-during-cold-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive The U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government&#x27;s Secret Search for Foreign Objects...31 Jan 2018 — This CIA Directorate of Intelligence report set forth the results of the...</span></span></span>

The indirect nature of these acquisitions also reduced escalation risks. A Soviet-made missile captured from an Egyptian unit or recovered in Vietnam could be studied without requiring a raid into Soviet territory. Proxy wars therefore became intelligence opportunities that neither superpower could easily create on its own.

## Middle East and Vietnam Collection Paths

### The Middle East as a Soviet Hardware Showcase

The Arab–Israeli wars became one of the richest sources of Soviet equipment available to Western analysts. Egypt and Syria operated extensive inventories of Soviet aircraft, tanks, missiles, radars and communications systems. Israeli battlefield victories produced large quantities of captured materiel, much of which was subsequently examined by American intelligence agencies.

After the 1967 Six-Day War, US officials moved quickly to exploit Soviet equipment captured by Israel. Declassified records show dedicated programmes established to study these systems, reflecting the belief that they could fill important intelligence gaps regarding Soviet design methods and military capabilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/search?op=Search&amp;page=285&amp;s=The+Cold+War" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive Search</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveSearch - National Security ArchiveFile Date Jun 20, 1967 Description The Israelis captured an enormous amount of...</span></span></span>

The process expanded after later Arab–Israeli conflicts. Captured inventories included tanks, anti-aircraft systems, anti-tank missiles, communications equipment and engineering vehicles. For Washington, the value extended beyond identifying individual weapons. Analysts gained insight into Soviet production quality, design philosophy and technological priorities across multiple military sectors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jta.org/archive/israel-discloses-nature-of-soviet-weapons-captured-during-the-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jta.org">[jta.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jta.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">israel discloses nature of soviet weapons captured during the war</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jewish Telegraphic AgencyIsrael Discloses Nature of Soviet Weapons Captured...11 Dec 1973 — Millions of rounds of ammunition, anti-tank...</span></span></span>

The Middle East thus functioned as an indirect access route into Soviet military technology. Instead of examining export brochures or intelligence estimates, Western specialists could dismantle actual equipment that had seen combat.

### Vietnam and the Search for Air-Defence Secrets

Vietnam created a different but equally important collection pathway. North Vietnam received extensive Soviet military aid, including the SA-2 surface-to-air missile system and associated Fan Song radar networks. These systems posed a direct threat to American aircraft and quickly became high-priority intelligence targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/media_mentions/2020-02-05-the_soviet_union_loved_these_secret_weapons_and_so_did_the_cia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2020 02 05 the soviet union loved these secret weapons and so did the cia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveThe Soviet Union Loved These Secret Weapons (And So...5 Feb 2020 — Nonetheless, the United States eventually ga...</span></span></span>

American interest was not merely academic. Aircraft losses created urgent pressure to understand how Soviet air-defence technology worked in practice. Captured missile components, radar equipment and associated electronics helped engineers develop jamming techniques, warning systems and operational tactics designed to reduce vulnerability.

Declassified accounts indicate that access to Soviet equipment obtained through allied captures and regional conflicts helped close important intelligence gaps connected to the war in Southeast Asia. The goal was to understand not only the missile itself but the broader technological ecosystem behind it, including radar performance, production standards and maintenance approaches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/media_mentions/2020-02-05-the_soviet_union_loved_these_secret_weapons_and_so_did_the_cia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2020 02 05 the soviet union loved these secret weapons and so did the cia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveThe Soviet Union Loved These Secret Weapons (And So...5 Feb 2020 — Nonetheless, the United States eventually ga...</span></span></span>

### Korea and the First Major Harvest

The Korean War demonstrated early in the Cold War how proxy conflicts could become technical intelligence opportunities. Soviet-designed MiG-15 fighters operated by Communist forces represented a major challenge to Western air power. Their performance generated intense interest among American analysts and pilots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/16252-document-01-mig-15s-korea-excerpt-far" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive Mi G-15s from Korea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Excerpt from Far East Air Force Weekly...31 Jan 2018 — The MiG-15 was a capable fighter during the Korean War. High on the priority list...</span></span></span>

US, British and South Korean personnel conducted recovery operations on crashed MiG-15s, sometimes salvaging substantial portions of the aircraft. These efforts provided rare opportunities to examine one of the most advanced fighters available to Communist forces. The quest for a flyable MiG became so important that the United States launched programmes encouraging [defections]({{ 'defections/' | relative_url }}) in exchange for rewards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/16252-document-01-mig-15s-korea-excerpt-far" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive Mi G-15s from Korea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Excerpt from Far East Air Force Weekly...31 Jan 2018 — The MiG-15 was a capable fighter during the Korean War. High on the priority list...</span></span></span>

Korea established a pattern repeated throughout the Cold War: regional warfare exposed advanced equipment, and intelligence organisations rapidly mobilised to secure it before it disappeared or was destroyed.

## What Indirect Access Changed

The most significant consequence of proxy-war acquisitions was not simple imitation. Captured systems allowed analysts to replace assumptions with measurements. Engineers could test radar frequencies, inspect manufacturing methods, assess component quality and evaluate claims about performance.

Several important changes followed:

## The Hidden Infrastructure of the Hardware Chase

Viewed through the lens of reverse engineering, proxy wars were more than geopolitical contests. They formed a hidden infrastructure that moved military technology across political boundaries. Every battlefield defeat, abandoned missile battery or recovered aircraft created an opportunity for technical exploitation.

This indirect flow of hardware helped shape Cold War intelligence. Korean crash sites, Vietnamese air-defence networks and Middle Eastern battlefields repeatedly delivered machines that the superpowers could not easily obtain through direct confrontation. The result was a global hardware chase in which regional wars supplied many of the most important technical prizes, allowing rival powers to dissect each other's technology without ever fighting a large-scale war against one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence/2018-01-31/scavenging-intelligence-us-governments-secret-search-foreign-objects-during-cold-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive The U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government&#x27;s Secret Search for Foreign Objects...31 Jan 2018 — This CIA Directorate of Intelligence report set forth the results of the...</span></span></span>
